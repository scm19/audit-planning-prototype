# Problem Memo: Where should a Single Audit team look first?

Stephen, 2026-10-19

## Executive summary

**Managers need to choose what staff will check before they have all this year’s facts.**

Before a Single Audit, the manager decides which federal programs and rules staff will test. Past reports can help, but useful details are spread across many years and organizations. The manager must find relevant history, decide what it means for this nonprofit, and give staff a clear focus. Staff then check this year’s records.

The question is how managers can reach a sound plan faster and feel confident in their choices.

## The challenges in Single Audits

**Managers must plan before they have all the facts.**

A nonprofit that spends $1 million or more in federal grants and other federal funds in a year must get a Single Audit ([2 CFR 200.501](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/2/200.501)). An independent accounting firm checks how the nonprofit used that money. The finished report is published in the [Federal Audit Clearinghouse](https://www.fac.gov), a government database. Auditors call a reported problem a “finding.”

1. **Auditors check some programs more closely than others.** Federal rules tell them how to choose these “major programs” ([2 CFR 200.518](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/2/200.518)). Problems in other programs may go unreported.
2. **The plan comes early.** The nonprofit’s spending totals may not be final. Last year’s audit may not be public yet. Gateway’s 2021 report, for example, reached the Clearinghouse in April 2024.
3. **Past reports are hard to use quickly.** The Clearinghouse has more than 400,000 audits. Each report lists programs, problems auditors found and the nonprofit’s response. But it shows only what auditors checked and reported. Comparing reports across nonprofits takes time.

## Why start with the audit manager

**The manager decides where the team spends its time.**

| Role | What they do | Control over the plan |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Partner | Reviews the plan and signs the final report. | Approves the plan. |
| **Manager** | Plans and oversees several audits; chooses where staff should focus ([Aprio](https://jobs.lever.co/Aprio/705d6e91-0dd0-4097-bae2-768a2c252f1d), [Plante Moran](https://careers.plantemoran.com/audit-manager-single-audit-mumbai-india/job/B90C06BB9DB1955E9781CAED43C9CB1A)). | **Chooses testing priorities and directs staff.** |
| Staff | Ask for records, check samples and write up problems they find. | Carry out the plan. |

The manager makes three choices:

1. **Which programs should get a close look?** Federal rules require some large programs. The manager decides whether to add others.
2. **Which areas within each program need a closer look?** If a past audit found a problem with a program’s grant reports, the manager might make those reports an area for staff to examine closely.
3. **What should staff ask the nonprofit for now?** Early questions and document requests can reveal issues before staff begin their detailed checks.

The partner reviews the manager’s plan, and staff carry it out. The manager updates it when final spending numbers or new facts arrive.

## The audit manager’s journey today

**Past audits can help most while the manager chooses the first areas to check.**

The manager must turn scattered history into clear priorities before staff begin checking current-year records.

| Step | What the manager does | What makes it hard |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **1. Pick programs** | Choose which programs get a close look. | Spending totals are not final. Reports from other nonprofits take time to compare. |
| **2. Pick rules** | Choose rules that could have a significant effect on each selected program ([2 CFR 200.514](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/2/200.514)). | The federal guide to program rules and past findings take time to read together. |
| **3. Ask for records early** | Choose questions and documents; a senior auditor sends the requests. | Last year’s request list may miss this year’s concerns. |
| **Later: final numbers, detailed checks and findings** | Revise the plan, review staff work and send any reported problems to the partner. | Past audits help less once the nonprofit’s current records arrive. |

## Why focus on setting the plan

**A past problem makes another problem more likely, but cannot tell us what happened this year.**

For 2018–2024 audits, 49% reported a program problem when the latest earlier report had one. Only 9% did when that earlier report had none. This comparison used only reports public by the end of the year being audited. In the same [Clearinghouse data](https://www.fac.gov/data/download/current/), 11,685 of 40,487 distinct program findings (29%) were marked as [repeats](https://www.fac.gov/data/download/current-dictionary/). Staff still need to check this year’s records.

**What needs proof:** How much time do managers spend finding and reading past reports? Which parts of that work slow them down? If past reports help more after current records arrive, the better focus may be the detailed audit work.

## The opportunity

**The problem to solve is the time managers spend turning past reports into a clear plan for staff.**

**For the audit firm:** Less time spent gathering history could help managers give staff clearer priorities with more confidence.

**For a network of audit firms:** Helping audit firms reduce this planning burden could let managers take on more work with confidence. Actual audit volume also depends on staff, partners and client demand.

## How to know it worked

**Measure manager time saved, plan quality, and work passed to audit staff.**

- Measure how long managers take to turn research into written testing priorities.
- Have another auditor review those priorities and required tests. On past audits, check whether the focus lined up with later reported problems.
- Track areas chosen with weak support, plus manager and audit staff hours separately. Faster planning should not move the work to staff.

## What must be validated

**Check who plans the work, how long research takes and whether history helps.**

1. **Who reads past reports and directs testing?** Confirm that managers choose the first areas and staff do the checks.
2. **How long does past-audit research take?** Watch managers plan and note which reports and other sources they use.
3. **Does history point to useful, specific checks?** Review past cases without using what auditors eventually found to set the initial focus. Compare with simple rules, such as repeating prior findings.
